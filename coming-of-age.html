Coming of age.

Many cultures mark the step from child to adult with a ceremony, a party or a new responsibility.

World and cultureTopic 6 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: Rites of passage that welcome a young person into adult life, in faith or in the community.

Why it matters: These are some of the proudest days in a family's life, and knowing what they mean helps you celebrate well.

Do this first

  1. Ask the family what the day means to them before you go.
  2. Dress up. Many of these events are formal.
  3. Bring a card or small gift if that is the custom. Ask if unsure.
  4. Praise the young person's effort. Many study or practice for months.
  5. Read up on what changes legally when you turn 18.

What happens

  • Bar and bat mitzvah. The name means "son (or daughter) of the commandment." The young person reads from the Torah at a service, usually on the Sabbath, and takes on adult religious duties. A blessing over wine and a family meal or party often follow.
  • First Communion and confirmation. Catholic children first receive Communion at about 7 or 8, after their first confession. Confirmation strengthens the faith of someone already baptized and follows classes in the faith. A bishop usually leads it. Orthodox Christians receive both as babies.
  • Quinceañera. A girl's 15th birthday in Mexico, Latin America, the Caribbean and Latino communities. It often starts with a Mass, then a party with a court of friends and a waltz-style dance. Some girls take a trip instead.
  • Coming of Age Day (Seijin no Hi). A national holiday in Japan on the second Monday of January. Young people dress in suits or kimono for city ceremonies. Japan lowered the legal adult age from 20 to 18 on April 1, 2022, but many cities still hold the ceremony at 20. Drinking and smoking are still for age 20 and up.
  • Amrit. The Sikh initiation, or "nectar ceremony," into the Khalsa. Guru Gobind Singh began it, and it is open to anyone who wishes to join.
  • Sacred thread (upanayana). A Hindu boy receives a loop of three cotton strands, at the upanayana initiation rite. It is usually worn over the left shoulder and across the chest for life.

Modern milestones

Many young people today also count everyday firsts as coming of age: a school graduation, a first job, a driver's license, a first vote, or moving out. These deserve a celebration too.

See turning 18, cars and driving, voting and work.

Everywhere

Rites differ in age and meaning, and families inside one faith may celebrate in different ways or not at all. What they share is a community saying, "We see you growing up, and we are proud of you."

In the United States

A coming-of-age ceremony does not change your legal age. The law sets its own ages for voting, contracts, driving and more. See turning 18 for what changes.
