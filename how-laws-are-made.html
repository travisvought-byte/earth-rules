How laws are made.

Laws start as ideas, and you can have a say at every step.

CivicTopic 4 of 6

In 10 seconds

What it is: A law is a rule written and passed by elected lawmakers, or in some places by voters themselves.

Why it matters: Laws decide your taxes, your schools, your roads and your rights, so knowing how they are made shows you where to push.

Do this first

  1. Find out who represents you in Congress, in your state and in your town.
  2. Look up a bill by its number to see where it is and who supports it.
  3. Contact your lawmaker while the bill is still in committee.
  4. Go to a local council or school board meeting. They are open to the public.
  5. Vote on ballot issues, not just candidates.

How a bill becomes a law in Congress

  1. A member of the House or Senate introduces a bill. It gets a number, like H.R. 123.
  2. The bill goes to a committee. The committee can change it, send it on, or set it aside. Most bills stop here.
  3. The full House votes. It needs a simple majority, which is 218 of 435 members.
  4. The Senate does the same. It needs a simple majority, which is 51 of 100 senators.
  5. If the two versions differ, members from both sides work out one final version, and both chambers approve it.
  6. The President has 10 days to sign it or veto it. Congress can still pass it over a veto with a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate.

Your local government

Many rules that touch your daily life are made close to home. City or village councils set local rules and budgets. County commissioners run county services. School boards decide how your schools are run and spend money.

These groups meet in public. Check their website for the meeting schedule and agenda, and go speak up when something matters to you. See contact your representative for how.

Everywhere

Almost every country has a parliament or legislature that writes its laws. The Inter-Parliamentary Union counts 188 national parliaments. Some have one chamber and some have two, like the US Congress.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights says everyone has the right to take part in the government of their country, directly or through representatives they freely choose.

In the United States

Federal laws are made by Congress, which has two parts: the House of Representatives and the Senate. Every state also has its own legislature that makes state laws, and cities and counties make local rules. A federal law applies everywhere in the country. A state law applies only in that state.

In Ohio

The Ohio General Assembly has two parts: the Ohio Senate with 33 members and the Ohio House with 99 members. A bill goes through committee hearings, where citizens can testify, then needs a majority vote in each chamber. The governor can sign it or veto it. If the governor does nothing for 10 days, it becomes law without a signature. Lawmakers can override a veto with a three-fifths vote in both chambers. A signed law usually takes effect after 90 days.

Citizens can write laws too. Ohio voters can put an issue on the ballot by petition. Each kind starts with at least 1,000 signatures sent to the Ohio Attorney General. Then you need many more, based on the number of votes cast for governor in the last election:

  • Constitutional amendment: signatures equal to 10% of that vote, with at least 5% from each of 44 of Ohio's 88 counties.
  • New law (initiated statute): 3% of that vote, with at least 1.5% from each of 44 counties.
  • Referendum to stop a new law: 6% of that vote, with at least 3% from each of 44 counties.

Local meetings are open. Ohio law says meetings of public bodies, like city councils, county commissioners and school boards, are open to the public at all times.
