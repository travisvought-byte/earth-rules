World religions.

About 3 in 4 people on Earth belong to a religion, and about 1 in 4 do not.

World and cultureTopic 3 of 8

In 10 seconds

What it is: A side-by-side look at the world's largest religions, and at people with no religion, described in their own terms.

Why it matters: Knowing the basics helps you be a good neighbor, coworker and guest, and makes the news and history easier to follow.

Do this first

  1. Ask people about their faith with curiosity, not to argue or convince.
  2. Before you visit a place of worship, ask your host what to wear and expect.
  3. Before you cook for guests, ask about food rules. Don't guess.
  4. Tell your boss early if your faith needs a schedule change or time off.
  5. Give people with no religion the same respect.

That is about 2.3 billion Christians, 1.9 billion people with no religion, 1.2 billion Hindus, 324 million Buddhists and 14.8 million Jews. Every group is very diverse inside. Two people of the same faith may pray, eat and celebrate in very different ways.

The largest faiths in brief

  • Christianity. Christians follow the life and teachings of Jesus Christ, who lived about 2,000 years ago. Holy book: the Bible. The largest groups are the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox churches and the Protestant churches. Big holy days: Christmas and Easter.
  • Islam. Islam began in Mecca in the 7th century. Muslims believe God, called Allah, revealed his teachings, and that Muhammad is God's final prophet. Holy book: the Qur'an. About 80% of Muslims are Sunni and about 15% are Shia. Holy times: the month of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Food: many Muslims follow halal food rules, and most say a Muslim should not eat pork.
  • Hinduism. One of the oldest living religions, with roots going back at least to the 1500s BCE. Many Hindus believe in Brahman, the ultimate reality; karma; the cycle of rebirth, called samsara; and freedom from that cycle, called moksha. Vishnu, Shiva and the goddess Durga are widely worshipped. Texts include the Vedas and the Bhagavad Gita. Holy days include Diwali and Holi. Food: in a 2021 Pew survey in India, 44% of Hindus were vegetarian, and most said a person cannot be Hindu if they eat beef.
  • Buddhism. Founded in India nearly 2,500 years ago on the teachings of Gautama, the Buddha. Buddhists stress nonviolence and ethical living. The two main branches are Theravada and Mahayana. Holy day: Vesak. Food: many Buddhists are vegetarian, but not all. In the 2021 Pew survey in India, 25% were.
  • Judaism. Jews believe in one God who created the world and chose the Jewish people in a covenant, shown through the Torah. The core statement of faith, the Shema, says, "Hear, O Israel! the Lord is our God, the Lord alone." Holy days include Shabbat each week, Passover and Hanukkah. Food: kosher rules forbid pork, shellfish and fish without fins and scales, and keep meat and dairy apart. How strictly people keep kosher varies.
  • Other faiths. Sikhism grew from the teachings of Guru Nanak and nine Gurus after him, in India in the 1500s. Daoism, from China, centers on living in harmony with the natural order. Shinto comes from Japan. Jains are known for strict vegetarianism: 92% are vegetarian in the Pew India survey.

Being a respectful guest

  • Ask your host ahead of time what to wear and what will happen. Customs differ from place to place, even within one faith.
  • Modest clothes are a safe choice.
  • Be ready to take off your shoes or cover your head. In a Sikh gurdwara, everyone covers their head and goes barefoot.
  • Follow what others do. It is fine to sit quietly during prayers you don't join.
  • Silence your phone, and ask before you take photos.
  • If you are offered a meal, you may accept. Every gurdwara has a langar, a shared kitchen and dining hall.

No religion

About 1.9 billion people do not identify with any religion. This includes atheists, agnostics and people who say "nothing in particular." Some still hold spiritual beliefs or keep some practices. In 2020, people with no religion were the majority in 10 countries or territories, including China, Japan, the Czech Republic, the Netherlands, Uruguay and New Zealand.

Having no religion deserves the same respect as having one.

Everywhere

Religion shapes food, clothing, holidays, weddings and funerals in every part of the world. When you are unsure about a custom, the kindest move is to ask the person, not to assume. See also mourning customs and coming-of-age rites.

In the United States

Freedom of religion. The First Amendment to the Constitution begins: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." The government may not set up an official religion or stop you from practicing yours.

At work. Federal law covers employers with 15 or more workers. Your employer must make reasonable changes for your sincerely held religious, ethical or moral beliefs, unless it would be a substantial burden on the business. Examples: flexible schedules, shift swaps, job changes, and exceptions to dress and grooming rules. Harassment and firing because of religion are illegal. You usually have 180 days to file a charge, longer in some states. Call the EEOC at 1-800-669-4000. See workers' rights.
