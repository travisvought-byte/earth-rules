Grief.

Grief is the price of love, and there is no right or wrong way to go through it.

PeopleTopic 5 of 6

In 10 seconds

What it is: What grief feels like, how to take care of yourself, and how to help someone who is grieving.

Why it matters: Almost everyone loses someone, and knowing what's normal makes it less frightening for you and the people around you.

Do this first

  1. Let yourself feel it. Good days and bad days are both normal.
  2. Eat, sleep and move a little, even when you don't want to.
  3. Let people help with meals, chores and errands.
  4. Put off big life decisions if you can.
  5. If grief makes daily life hard for a long time, talk to a doctor or counselor.

Numbers to call

  • 988Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text

What grief feels like

Grief doesn't move in neat stages. It's more like a roller coaster. You might feel:

  • Sad, numb, shocked or scared
  • Angry, even at the person who died
  • Guilty, or relieved, or both
  • Tired, with trouble sleeping, eating or thinking clearly

All of these are normal. Grief can also be hard on your body, so take care of your health.

How to help a grieving friend

  • Say something simple: "I'm so sorry. I'm here for you." Use the name of the person who died.
  • Skip the clichés: avoid "It's going to be okay," "They're in a better place," or "It was their time."
  • Offer something specific instead of "Let me know if you need anything." Bring a meal, mow the lawn, pick up family from the airport.
  • Listen. You don't have to fix it. Sitting together quietly helps.
  • Keep showing up. Reach out months later and on birthdays, holidays and the anniversary.

When grief needs more help

For most people grief eases over time. For some, it stays intense and gets in the way of life. Doctors call this prolonged grief, and it can be treated. Talk to a doctor or counselor if, after a long time, you can't accept the loss, can't make plans, or can't manage daily things like eating and getting dressed. Grief support groups and counseling help many people. Hospice programs often offer grief support too.

If you have thoughts of not wanting to be alive, call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org (opens in a new tab). Veterans can press 1.

The paperwork

When someone dies there are calls to make and papers to file. See when someone dies for a step-by-step list.

Everywhere

Every culture has its own ways to mourn and remember. Rituals, faith, art, time outdoors and simply talking about the person can all help.

In the United States

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free and open 24/7 by call, text or chat. SAMHSA says grief support groups, counseling and peer support are all good options.
