Cooking cheap.

Eating well on a tight budget is about planning, a few staples, and keeping food safe.

Skills and funTopic 1 of 4

In 10 seconds

What it is: These are simple ways to cook healthy meals for less, and the food safety rules that keep you from getting sick.

Why it matters: Cooking at home with cheap staples stretches every dollar, and safe food handling prevents food poisoning.

Do this first

  1. Plan the week's meals and make a list before you shop.
  2. Buy store brands and compare unit prices on the shelf tag.
  3. Stock dried beans, brown rice, oats and big bags of frozen vegetables.
  4. Put leftovers in the fridge within 2 hours.
  5. Eat leftovers within 4 days, and reheat to 165°F.

Shop smart

USDA's money-saving tips:

  • Plan your weekly meals and snacks, so you buy only what you need and use up leftovers.
  • Store brands are the same quality and cost less.
  • Unit prices on the shelf tag show the real cost per ounce or pound. Larger sizes often cost less per serving, if you can use them before they spoil.
  • Frozen and canned fruits and vegetables last longer and are budget-friendly, especially out of season. Pick canned foods marked low sodium or no salt added.

Cheap staples

  • Dried beans and peas: a good source of protein and fiber, and they keep a year or more.
  • Brown rice, old-fashioned oats and grits: buy regular, not instant. They cost less.
  • Big bags of frozen vegetables.
  • Seasonal fresh produce, which is cheaper in season.

Keep it safe

FoodSafety.gov's four steps are clean, separate, cook and chill.

  • Cook to a safe temperature (use a food thermometer): chicken and turkey 165°F, ground meat 160°F, steaks, chops and fish 145°F, egg dishes 160°F, leftovers 165°F.
  • The 2-hour rule: never leave perishable food out more than 2 hours. Above 90°F, like a hot car or picnic, make it 1 hour.
  • Keep your fridge at 40°F or below.
  • Eat leftovers within 4 days, or freeze them.

Help buying food

SNAP (food stamps), WIC for families with young kids, and food banks can help. See basics. To read labels, see food labels.

Everywhere

Everywhere, planning meals, cooking at home, and using beans, grains and vegetables keeps food costs down. Keep cold food cold and hot food hot.

In the United States

In the US, the USDA and FoodSafety.gov publish free guides on budget meals and safe cooking temperatures.
