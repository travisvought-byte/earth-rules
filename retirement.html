Retirement.

Retirement is paid for by Social Security plus what you save. Starting early makes the biggest difference.

Your timelineTopic 7 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: Retirement is when you stop working full time and live on Social Security, a pension, and your savings.

Why it matters: Social Security alone replaces only about 40% of what people earned, so your own savings fill the gap.

Do this first

  1. Create a my Social Security account and look at your benefit estimate.
  2. Put enough in your work plan to get the full employer match.
  3. No plan at work? Open an IRA, even with a small amount.
  4. Check the fees on every account you have.
  5. Check out anyone who sells you an investment before you buy.

Numbers to call

  • 1-800-772-1213Social Security

Social Security

  • Age 62 is the earliest you can start. Your check is cut, by up to about 30%, and the cut is permanent.
  • Age 67 is full retirement age for anyone born in 1960 or later. You get your full benefit.
  • Waiting past 67 adds 8% for each year, until age 70. Starting at 70 gives about 24% more per month for life. There is no gain in waiting past 70.

Create a free my Social Security account at ssa.gov to check your work record and see your estimate at different ages.

Medicare starts at 65. Your sign up window is 7 months: 3 months before your 65th birthday month, that month, and 3 months after. Missing it can mean a penalty that lasts as long as you have Part B. See coverage.

Saving on your own

  • 401(k) or 403(b): a plan through your job. Money comes out of your paycheck. Many employers add a match. That is free money, so put in at least enough to get all of it.
  • IRA: an account you open yourself at a bank or investment company.
  • Traditional: you may get a tax break now, and you pay tax when you take money out.
  • Roth: you pay tax now, and qualified withdrawals later are tax free.

Taking money out before age 59 and a half usually costs an extra 10% tax. When you change jobs, leave your savings in the plan or roll it into an IRA or your new plan instead of cashing out.

A bonus for lower incomes

Saver's Credit (2026): If you save in a retirement plan or IRA, you may get a tax credit for part of what you put in. For 2026, your income must be no more than $40,250 if single, $60,375 for head of household, or $80,500 if married filing jointly.

Saver's Match (starts with 2027 savings): The government will put up to $1,000 a year straight into your retirement account, matching up to 50% of what you save. It replaces the Saver's Credit for retirement accounts. See taxes.

Watch out for fees and scams

Fees add up. The SEC shows $100,000 growing 4% a year for 20 years. With a 0.25% yearly fee you end with about $208,000. With a 1% fee you end with about $179,000. Ask what every fee is and look for low cost funds.

Scam warning signs: a promise that sounds too good to be true, pressure to decide fast, a "once in a lifetime" deal, someone who tells you to leave everything to them, or trouble getting your own money back. Look up any broker on FINRA BrokerCheck or the SEC's free search at Investor.gov before you invest. See scams.

Everywhere

The sooner you start saving, the more time your money has to grow. Start with a small amount and raise it over time. Experts estimate you will need about 70 to 80% of your income before retirement to keep the same way of life.

In the United States

Social Security pays retirement benefits based on your work record. On average it replaces about 40% of what people earned before retiring. The rest comes from a pension if you have one, your 401(k) or IRA, and other savings. See money for budgeting and saving basics.

In Ohio

Ohio public jobs have their own retirement systems instead of Social Security for that job: OPERS for most state and local government workers, STRS Ohio for teachers, SERS for school staff, the Ohio Police and Fire Pension Fund, and the Highway Patrol Retirement System. Ask your system for your pension estimate.
