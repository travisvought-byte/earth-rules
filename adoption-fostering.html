Adoption and foster care.

Thousands of kids need a safe home for a while, or for good, and many kinds of people can give them one.

Love and familyTopic 7 of 9

In 10 seconds

What it is: Foster care is a temporary home for a child who cannot safely live with their parents. Adoption makes a child a permanent, legal part of a new family.

Why it matters: You do not need to be rich, married or a homeowner to foster or adopt, and help exists for relatives already raising a child.

Do this first

  1. Contact your county children services agency or a licensed private agency.
  2. Take the required pre-placement training.
  3. Complete a home study, background checks and a fire safety check.
  4. If you adopt, ask about adoption assistance and the federal adoption tax credit.
  5. If you are raising a relative's child, ask for kinship support.

How foster care works

Foster care is meant to be temporary. The main goal is to get the child safely back with their own family, and the family gets services to help. If a child cannot go home, the next best choice is usually a relative or close family friend, called kin. If neither works, a court may choose guardianship or adoption. Do not foster only to adopt, because reunification comes first.

Ways to adopt

Types of adoption and typical costs
TypeTypical cost
From foster careAlmost free. Many kids qualify for monthly help and Medicaid.
Private agencyAbout $30,000 to $60,000
Independent, through a lawyerAbout $25,000 to $45,000
Another countryAbout $20,000 to $50,000

Stepparent and relative adoptions go through the courts. The rules, including whether a home study is needed, vary by state.

Adopting from another country usually falls under the Hague Adoption Convention, which protects children from being sold or trafficked. You must use a provider accredited or approved by the US government, and it must tell you its fees in writing up front.

Help with costs

The federal adoption tax credit helps pay qualified adoption costs. For tax year 2026 it is up to $17,670 per child, and up to $5,120 of that can come back to you as a refund even if you owe no tax. If a state finds the child has special needs, you can claim the full credit even with no costs. Unused credit can carry forward up to 5 years. See taxes.

Kinship care: grandparents and relatives

Many grandparents, aunts, uncles and family friends raise a child when parents cannot. Children's agencies see relatives as the next best home after a child's own parents. Ask your county children services agency, and see aging and caregiving and basics.

Everywhere

Child welfare experts agree that a child does best in a safe, permanent family. Keeping a child with their own family comes first, then relatives, then adoption. Adoption across borders should only happen when a suitable family cannot be found in the child's own country.

In the United States

Child Welfare Information Gateway (opens in a new tab) (childwelfare.gov) explains foster care and adoption state by state. AdoptUSKids (opens in a new tab) lists children in foster care waiting for families and connects you to your state's process.

In Ohio

Foster parents: You must be at least 18. You can be single, married or co-parents. You need enough income for your household's basic needs, background checks for every adult in the home, and a fire safety inspection.

Relatives raising kids: OhioKAN, the Ohio Kinship and Adoption Navigator, helps formal and informal kin caregivers and adoptive families find support, like child care help, short breaks for caregivers, training and legal help.

Aging out: Young adults who leave foster care at 18 can join Bridges until age 21, if they are in school, working at least 80 hours a month, in a job program, or unable to because of a health condition. Bridges helps with housing, school, jobs, budgeting and health care. It is voluntary.
