EARTH RULES · SPOT THE TRICK

Spot the trick.

The tricks people use on you, and on themselves. Name it and it stops working.

BrainTopic 3 of 7

Name it and it loses power

Everyone uses these tricks: sellers, scammers, politicians, preachers, bosses, friends and you. Most of the time nobody plans it. Once you can name a trick, it's much harder to fall for it.

Persuasion tricks

  • Fake urgency

    "Today only." "Only 2 left." A countdown clock. Rushing you stops you from thinking or asking anyone.

    Your move: Anything real will still be there tomorrow. Sleep on it.

  • Scarcity

    Things seem worth more when there's less of them, even when the shortage is made up.

    Your move: Ask what it's worth to you if there were a thousand of them.

  • Reciprocity

    A free gift, sample or favor makes you feel you owe something back.

    Your move: You can say thank you and still say no.

  • Authority

    Uniforms, titles, logos and "experts say" make people obey without checking.

    Your move: Ask what the person actually knows, and who pays them.

  • Social proof

    "Everyone's buying it." Fake reviews, paid crowds and follower counts.

    Your move: Ask how many people, and who counted.

  • Foot in the door

    A small yes first, then a bigger ask. People want to stay consistent with what they already agreed to.

    Your move: Each new ask is a new decision.

Propaganda moves

  • Us versus them

    Name an enemy group and blame them for your problems. The fastest way to win loyalty.

    Your move: Ask whether the group is really one thing, and who gains if you hate it.

  • Repetition

    Say it again and again. Things we hear often start to feel true, even when they aren't.

    Your move: Ask where you first heard it, and whether anyone showed proof.

  • Fear

    Make people scared enough to stop asking questions.

    Your move: Scared is when to slow down, not speed up.

  • Name-calling and labels

    A nickname or label replaces an argument.

    Your move: Ask what the person actually said or did.

  • False choice

    "You're either with us or against us." Only two options, when there are many.

    Your move: Ask what the third and fourth options are.

  • Cherry-picking

    Only the facts that help, none that hurt.

    Your move: Ask what's been left out.

  • Whataboutism

    Answering a question by pointing at someone else's bad act.

    Your move: Both can be wrong. Bring it back to the question.

Shortcuts our brains take

These are called cognitive biases. Everyone has them. They save time and they cost money.

  • Confirmation bias

    We notice what fits what we already believe and skip what doesn't.

    Your move: Look for the best case against your view.

  • Sunk cost

    "I've already put so much into it." Staying in a bad job, deal or relationship because of what's already spent.

    Your move: What's spent is gone either way. Decide from today forward.

  • Loss aversion

    Losing $100 feels about twice as bad as winning $100 feels good, so we take bad bets to avoid a loss.

    Your move: Ask what you'd choose if you were starting fresh.

  • Anchoring

    The first number you hear sets the range. That's why sticker prices are high.

    Your move: Find out the real price before you hear theirs.

  • Availability

    Whatever's on the news feels more common than it is.

    Your move: Look up the actual numbers.

  • Halo effect

    Good-looking, confident or famous people seem right about everything.

    Your move: Judge the claim, not the person.

Groups, faith and why minds don't change

People hold beliefs that tie them to their family, faith, team or party. Changing one of those beliefs can feel like leaving the group, so the mind protects it. Facts rarely change someone's mind in one conversation; studies find people do shift a little when the facts don't threaten who they are.

  • Every group, religious or not, has insiders and outsiders. Notice when a rule applies only to them.
  • If you want to change a mind, start with what you share, ask questions, and give the person a way to save face.
  • If you want to keep your own mind open, say out loud: "I might be wrong about this." See how to disagree well.

Strange things people do

  • Going along with the room. In the 1950s Asch experiments, many people gave an answer they could see was wrong because everyone else did.
  • Obeying orders. In Milgram's experiments, most people kept giving what they believed were painful shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to.
  • The bystander effect. The more people who see an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help. If you need help, point at one person and tell them what to do.
  • Liking what's familiar. We tend to like things more just because we've seen them before. That's why ads repeat.
  • Valuing what we built. People value things more when they put work into them, even flat-pack furniture.
