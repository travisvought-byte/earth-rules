How people work.

Teams, tribes, betting, drinking and the unwritten rules, without the preaching.

PeopleTopic 2 of 9

People are built for groups

For most of history, being part of a group meant staying alive. That wiring is still in us. It makes us loyal, generous and brave. It also makes us easy to fool.

Teams and tribes

Sports fans, neighborhoods, churches, political parties and fan clubs all give people a team. Studies going back to the 1970s found people favor their own group even when the groups are made up on the spot by a coin toss.

That's normal. It turns into a problem when your team decides what's true, or who counts as a person.

Us versus them, and who profits

  • Anger and fear keep people watching and clicking, so news, ads and social media feeds lean into them.
  • Politicians, sellers and scammers all use the same move: name an enemy, then ask you to trust them.
  • Ask: who gains if I believe this? Who's left out of the story? Would I believe it if the other side said it?

Gambling

Every casino game, lottery and sports bet is priced so the house wins over time. Betting apps make it fast, private and always on, and ads link it to being a real fan. A win now and then is part of the design; it keeps you playing.

If you bet more than you planned, chase losses, or hide it, that's the sign. Help groups are free.

Alcohol

Many places treat drinking as normal or even expected. It's also one of the leading causes of preventable death in the US, and the US Surgeon General says it raises the risk of several cancers. Less is safer, and not drinking is a normal choice. If it's costing you, help groups are free.

Culture: the unwritten rules

Every place has rules nobody writes down: how close to stand, whether to look people in the eye, how much to tip, what time "on time" means, how direct to be, what you can joke about. None of them is the right way. They're just the local way.

  • When you're new somewhere, watch first. Do what people around you do.
  • Ask. "How do people usually do this here?" is a respectful question almost everywhere.
  • Assume good intent. Most clashes are two sets of rules, not two bad people.
