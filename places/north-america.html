North America.

If you're thinking about suicide or can't cope

  • United States: call or text 988. Veterans press 1.
  • Canada: call or text 9-8-8, any time, in English or French.
  • Mexico: Línea de la Vida, 800 911 2000.

For any other country, Find A Helpline (opens in a new tab) lists free, checked help lines in more than 150 countries. It's run by ThroughLine and backed by the International Association for Suicide Prevention.

Rights everyone has

In 1948 the United Nations agreed on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (opens in a new tab). It says every person, in every country, is born free and equal in dignity and rights. Countries don't all live up to it, but it's the common starting point.

If you're arrested away from home

You can ask the police to tell your country's consulate. They have to tell you that you can ask. This comes from a treaty almost every country has signed, the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations.

Rights in North America

Mexico has joined the American Convention on Human Rights, a treaty that lets people take a country to the Inter-American Court of Human Rights in San José, Costa Rica. The United States signed it in 1977 but never ratified it, and Canada hasn't joined. In those two countries, your rights come from their own constitutions and laws.

North America at a glance

The United Nations counts the United States and Canada together as "Northern America," with about 385 million people in 2024. It counts Mexico with Latin America and the Caribbean, so Mexico's people are not in that number.

Canada has two official languages, English and French. Mexico recognizes 69 national languages: Spanish and 68 Indigenous languages, spoken in 364 different varieties. The UN also counts about 183 million people in Central America and 44 million in the Caribbean (2024).
