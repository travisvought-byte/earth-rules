Food labels.

The box on the back of food tells you what is really inside, once you know where to look.

BodyTopic 4 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: The Nutrition Facts label is the black-and-white box the government requires on most packaged food.

Why it matters: It lets you compare two foods in seconds and spot the salt, sugar and fat you might not see.

Do this first

  1. Look at the serving size and how many servings are in the package first.
  2. Multiply everything on the label if you eat more than one serving.
  3. Use % Daily Value: 5% or less is low, 20% or more is high.
  4. Aim low on saturated fat, sodium and added sugars.
  5. Aim high on fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium.
  6. Read the ingredients list. The first ingredient is the one there is most of.

Serving size and calories

The serving size is the amount people usually eat. It is not a rule for how much you should eat. Every number on the label is for one serving. If you eat the whole bag and the bag has 3 servings, triple the calories and everything else.

The FDA uses 2,000 calories a day as a general guide. Your own needs may be higher or lower.

The 5 and 20 rule

The % Daily Value (%DV) column shows how much of a day's amount one serving gives you.

  • 5% or less is low.
  • 20% or more is high.

Pick foods low in what you want less of, and high in what you want more of. It also makes comparing two brands quick.

What to watch

  • Get less of: saturated fat, sodium (salt) and added sugars. Too much is linked to heart disease and high blood pressure.
  • Get more of: fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium. Most Americans do not get enough.
  • Added sugars are listed under total sugars. They are sugars put in during making, not the natural sugar in fruit or milk.

The daily amounts the label uses include 2,300 mg of sodium, 50 grams of added sugars, 20 grams of saturated fat and 28 grams of fiber.

The ingredients list

Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight. If sugar or salt is near the top, there is a lot of it. Short lists of foods you recognize are usually a good sign.

Everywhere

Many countries put nutrition panels on packaged food, but the layout and daily amounts differ. Comparing serving sizes and reading the ingredients list works anywhere.

In the United States

In the US, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) sets what goes on the Nutrition Facts label. Its free label guide (opens in a new tab) walks through each line. For help paying for food, see basics.
