EARTH RULES · MONEY

How money works.

Interest, credit, debt and the system, in plain words. And where to get free help.

Money and papersTopic 1 of 12

The short version

Money flows toward people who already have it. If you have money, you earn interest. If you need money, you pay interest. Most of the system is built on that one fact.

You can't change the system alone. You can stop paying it more than you have to.

Interest: the price of money

Interest is rent on money. When you borrow, you pay rent. When you save, the bank pays you rent, usually far less than it charges.

Compound interest means interest on top of interest. It works for you when you save and against you when you owe.

15 yearsto pay off $3,000 on a credit card at 22% if you pay only the minimum. Interest: about $4,400.
26 monthsto pay off the same $3,000 at $150 a month. Interest: about $770.
$69,000is what $100 a month grows to in 30 years at 4% a year. You put in $36,000.

The average credit card rate for people who carry a balance was about 22% in 2026, from the Federal Reserve. Our math assumes a minimum payment of the interest plus 1% of the balance, at least $25.

Credit scores: a grade for how you pay

Lenders, landlords and some employers look at a score built from your credit report. The FICO score weighs:

  • Paying on time: about 35%. One payment 30 days late can hurt for years.
  • How much you owe: about 30%. Using a small part of your limit helps.
  • How long you've had credit: about 15%. Keep old cards open if they're free.
  • New credit and the mix of credit: about 20%. Apply only when you need to.

See your credit reports free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com (opens in a new tab), the only official site. Fix mistakes by disputing them with the credit bureau.

The traps

  • Payday and title loans. A $15 fee for every $100 borrowed for two weeks works out to almost 400% a year, the CFPB says.
  • Minimum payments. They're set so you stay in debt longer. Pay more than the minimum whenever you can.
  • Overdraft fees. Turn off overdraft "coverage" so a card is declined instead of charged a fee.
  • Rent-to-own and buy now, pay later. Add up the total before you sign. It's often far more than the price tag.
  • Anything you don't understand. If someone can't explain where the money comes from in one sentence, keep your money.

How the big system works

Banks make money by lending. When a bank makes a loan, it creates new money in your account. That's how most money comes to exist, the Bank of England explains.

Central banks set the base price of money. In the US that's the Federal Reserve. When it raises rates, loans cost more for everyone. When prices rise faster than your pay, your money buys less. That's inflation.

Wealth piles up at the top. In the US, the richest 1% of households held about a third of all wealth in 2026, by the Federal Reserve's own count. That's about as much as the bottom 90% combined.

Every country works this way, with different rules for banks, debt and help. Your protections depend on where you live.

What actually works

  • Keep a little cash for emergencies, even $20 a week. It's what keeps a flat tire from becoming a payday loan.
  • Pay the highest-interest debt first, and never miss a minimum.
  • Take free money: an employer's retirement match, tax credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit, and benefits you qualify for.
  • Call your lender before you miss a payment. Many have hardship plans they don't advertise.

Free money help

  • Nonprofit credit counseling: 800-388-2227, the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (opens in a new tab). Free or low cost, and they work out payment plans.
  • Complaints and debt collectors: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (opens in a new tab). See also your rights as a consumer.
  • Free tax help: IRS VITA (opens in a new tab) for people with low to moderate income, people with disabilities and people who speak limited English.
  • Anything else: dial 211.
