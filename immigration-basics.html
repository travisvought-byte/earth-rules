Immigration basics.

Immigration rules are complicated, so get your information from official sources and real legal help.

Help nowTopic 6 of 6

In 10 seconds

What it is: A plain overview of how people come to live in the U.S., where to get trustworthy help, and how to avoid scams.

Why it matters: One wrong form or one bad adviser can cost years, so it pays to know who you can trust.

Do this first

  1. Use only websites that end in .gov for immigration forms and news.
  2. Get advice only from a licensed lawyer or a DOJ-accredited representative.
  3. Never pay a notario or consultant for immigration legal help.
  4. Never sign a blank form or anything you don't understand.
  5. Keep copies of everything you send.

Common paths

U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) lists many ways to get a green card (permanent residence). The most common are:

  • Family: spouses, children, parents and some other relatives of U.S. citizens or permanent residents
  • Work: certain workers, professionals and investors
  • Refugee or asylee status
  • Special cases: such as survivors of abuse, trafficking or certain crimes, and the diversity visa lottery

Becoming a U.S. citizen (naturalization) is a separate step. Official details are at uscis.gov (opens in a new tab).

Watch out for notario fraud

In many Latin American countries a notario is a lawyer. In the U.S., a notario público or notary public is not allowed to give immigration legal help. Only a licensed attorney, or an accredited representative working for an organization recognized by the Department of Justice, can give you legal advice. Some websites pretend to be USCIS, so check that the address ends in .gov.

Free or low-cost help

The Justice Department's immigration court office (EOIR) keeps a list of free legal service providers (opens in a new tab) and a roster of recognized organizations and accredited representatives, by state. Nonprofit legal aid can also help. Find it through LawHelp.org (opens in a new tab).

Your rights

The Constitution's guarantee of due process covers people in immigration court. That includes a fair chance to be represented by a lawyer. For what to do if police stop you, see police and your rights.

Everywhere

Every country sets its own rules for who can visit, work or live there. Moving abroad? See the world.

In the United States

USCIS handles applications for green cards and citizenship. Immigration courts are run by EOIR at the Department of Justice. This page is general information, not legal advice. Talk to a lawyer or accredited representative about your own case.
