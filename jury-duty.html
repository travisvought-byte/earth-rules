Jury duty.

Jury duty means ordinary people decide cases, and your job is protected while you serve.

CivicTopic 6 of 6

In 10 seconds

What it is: Jury duty is when a court calls you to help decide a trial as a juror.

Why it matters: A fair trial depends on regular people showing up, and ignoring a summons can get you fined.

Do this first

  1. Open the summons and read the date, court and instructions.
  2. Fill out the questionnaire by the deadline, online if the court offers it.
  3. Tell your employer right away and give them a copy.
  4. If the date won't work, ask the court to postpone. Don't just skip it.
  5. Check in the night before. Many trials settle and you may not need to go.

You can't ignore a summons

A jury summons is a court order. In federal court, a person who does not show up and has no good reason can be ordered before a judge and face a fine of up to $1,000, up to three days in jail, community service, or a mix of these.

If you can't make the date, contact the jury office on your summons. Courts often allow a new date for a conflict, a medical reason, or an emergency. Ask before your date, not after.

Watch for scams. If a caller threatens you over jury duty, hang up and call the court using the number on its official website.

Your job is protected

Federal law says an employer can't fire, threaten, or pressure a permanent employee for serving on a federal jury. An employer who breaks this law can owe lost wages and a civil penalty of up to $5,000 per violation. A court can also give you your job back.

Your employer does not have to keep paying you while you serve, unless your job policy says so. Ask HR.

What happens and what to bring

  • Bring your summons, a photo ID to get into the building, a sweater because courtrooms can be cold, and something to read.
  • Phones are allowed in some courts and not others. Check your summons.
  • Plan for a full day. You go through security, watch a short orientation, then may go to a courtroom.
  • Jury selection is when the judge and lawyers ask questions to find people who can be fair. Just answer honestly. If a question is too personal, you can ask to answer privately at the judge's bench.
  • Pay is small. Federal jurors get $50 a day, plus some travel costs. State court pay is set locally.

Everywhere

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights says everyone has the right to a fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial court. Countries use juries in different ways. If you live outside the US, your court notice or court website explains what is asked of you.

In the United States

Most US citizens 18 and older can be called. Federal courts pick names at random from people in the district. State and county courts run their own jury lists. Federal jurors are paid $50 a day and may get up to $60 a day after 10 days if the judge orders it.

In Ohio

  • Job protection. Ohio law says an employer can't fire, threaten to fire, or discipline a permanent employee called for jury duty, as long as you gave reasonable notice before you served. Your employer also can't make you use vacation or sick time for jury duty.
  • One easy postponement. If you ask at least two business days before your first day, by phone, in writing, or by email, the court must give you one postponement. You agree on a new date, usually within six months. Later postponements are only for real emergencies, like a death in the family or sudden illness.
  • Small employers. If your employer has 25 or fewer full-time workers and another worker served on a jury in the last 30 days, the court must move your date.
