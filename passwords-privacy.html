Passwords and privacy.

Four habits stop most online break-ins, and none of them need tech skills.

BrainTopic 7 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: This page covers strong passwords, two-step sign-in, spotting phishing, updates, backups and privacy settings.

Why it matters: Your email and phone hold the keys to your bank, benefits and photos, so locking them protects everything else.

Do this first

  1. Use a long, different password for every account, at least 16 characters.
  2. Use a password manager to make and store them.
  3. Turn on multifactor authentication, starting with email and banking.
  4. Don't click links in surprise messages. Go to the site yourself.
  5. Turn on automatic updates on your phone, computer and browser.

Passwords

The U.S. cybersecurity agency, CISA, says a strong password is:

  • Long: at least 16 characters. A passphrase of several random words is long and easy to remember.
  • Unique: never used anywhere else. If one site is hacked, the others stay safe.

A password manager makes and remembers them for you. Protect the manager itself with multifactor authentication.

Multifactor authentication (MFA) means you need more than a password to sign in, like a code or your fingerprint. It makes it much less likely you'll get hacked. Turn it on for email, social media, shopping and money accounts.

Phishing

Phishing is a message that tries to get you to click, open a file or share information. Signs:

  • Urgent or scary words, like "act now or lose your account"
  • Asks for personal or money information
  • Look-alike addresses, like "amazan.com"
  • Shortened links you can't read

Perfect spelling doesn't mean it's safe. AI writes clean messages now. Don't click, don't reply and don't use "unsubscribe." Report it and delete it. If you're unsure, contact the company using a website or number you already know. See scams.

Updates, backups and Wi-Fi

  • Updates fix holes criminals use. Don't put them off.
  • Back up photos and files often, to a cloud service or an external drive. Unplug the drive when you're not backing up, so ransomware can't reach it.
  • Public Wi-Fi is usually safe now because most sites encrypt. Look for the lock or "https" in the address bar.

Privacy

Check the privacy settings on your phone, browser, apps and smart TV. Turn off what you don't need.

Data brokers are companies that collect information about nearly everyone and sell it, mostly without people knowing. Some let you opt out. The FTC's guide at consumer.ftc.gov (opens in a new tab) explains how. See also identity theft.

Everywhere

The same four habits protect you anywhere: long unique passwords, a second sign-in step, care with links, and updates. They cost nothing.

In the United States

CISA's free Secure Our World (opens in a new tab) program teaches these four steps for families. The Federal Trade Commission explains public Wi-Fi (opens in a new tab) and privacy settings.
