Mourning customs.

Every culture honors its dead. Knowing a few customs helps you show up and comfort people well.

World and cultureTopic 7 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: How different faiths and cultures care for the dead, hold funerals and mourn.

Why it matters: When someone you know loses a loved one, knowing what to expect helps you support them without worry.

Do this first

  1. Go if you can. Showing up matters more than perfect words.
  2. Ask a friend of the family what to wear and what to expect.
  3. Keep it simple. "I'm so sorry" is enough.
  4. Before you bring food or flowers, ask what fits the family's customs.
  5. Check in again weeks later. Grief lasts long after the funeral.

Burial or cremation

  • Hindu families traditionally cremate. Ashes are usually scattered in a river, and some families take them to the Ganges in India.
  • Muslim families bury, and Islam asks that burial happen as soon as possible.
  • Jewish tradition is strongly against cremation, and Orthodox Judaism forbids it, though many Jews choose it.
  • Christian churches mostly allow both. Many Protestant churches support cremation, and the Roman Catholic Church says it is not forbidden.
  • In Japan, cremation is almost universal.

How soon the funeral happens

  • Jewish: traditional law calls for burial within 24 hours, with delays allowed so family can gather. Burial does not happen on the Sabbath. Many non-Orthodox families wait a few days.
  • Muslim: as soon as possible. Family or the local Islamic community washes and wraps the body.
  • Hindu: families try to cremate quickly, ideally before the next sunset.
  • Buddhist: many families want calm around the body and ask that it not be moved for at least four hours, so prayers can be said.

Mourning traditions

  • Sitting shiva. After a Jewish burial of a parent, spouse, child or sibling, mourners stay home for seven days. They may sit on low stools and cover mirrors. Friends visit to comfort them.
  • Hindu mourning often lasts 13 days. The family stays apart, cleans and purifies the home, and ends with a prayer service.
  • A wake is a watch over the body before burial, sometimes with festivity. The custom stayed strong in Ireland.
  • Day of the Dead, November 1 and 2, mainly in Mexico. Families build home altars, called ofrendas, with photos, candles, flowers and food, and decorate graves with marigolds. It is a time to remember loved ones with love, and often with humor and celebration.

Being a good guest

  • What to wear: plain, simple clothes are safe. Mourning colors differ between cultures, so ask.
  • Flowers: not part of Jewish mourning. Easy-to-serve food, a card, or a gift to charity in the person's memory are common instead. Ask about food rules first.
  • What to say: "I'm sorry" and a short memory of the person mean a lot. At a shiva home, some visitors wait for the mourner to speak first.
  • Keep showing up. Calls and meals in the weeks after can mean more than the funeral. See grief.

Everywhere

Customs vary inside every faith, and many families blend traditions. The family's wishes come first. When in doubt, ask someone close to the family, and follow what others do.
