Greetings and manners.

Good manners look different around the world, but paying attention and showing respect work everywhere.

World and cultureTopic 4 of 7

In 10 seconds

What it is: The unwritten rules for greeting people, visiting homes, eating, giving gifts, tipping and talking.

Why it matters: A few local customs help you make friends, do business and avoid hurting feelings by accident.

Do this first

  1. Watch what local people do, then follow their lead.
  2. Let the other person start the greeting: a handshake, bow, hug or kiss.
  3. Ask your host if you should take off your shoes.
  4. Look up tipping before you travel. It differs a lot.
  5. If you slip up, smile, say sorry and move on.

Greetings

Some common greetings (Michigan State University Extension)
PlaceCommon greeting
United StatesFirm handshake with eye contact. Hugs among friends.
MexicoBrisk handshake. Close friends may hug or kiss on the cheek.
PolandFirm handshake. Women and close friends may kiss three times on the cheeks.
JapanA bow. A lower bow shows more respect. A nod is fine in shops.
ChinaA bow or nod. A handshake, if offered, is not firm.
ParaguayA hug and a kiss on the right cheek, then the left.
SwedenA hearty handshake and "Hej."

These are common patterns, not rules for every person. Greetings change with age, region, faith and how well people know each other. When unsure, wait a moment and match what the other person offers.

Homes, shoes and the table

  • Shoes. In many homes, people take shoes off at the door. In Japan you switch to slippers at the entrance, take them off on tatami mats, and use separate slippers in the bathroom. In China, too, people often remove shoes when entering a home.
  • Forks. Americans often cut with the fork in the left hand, then switch it to the right to eat. In most of Europe, the fork stays in the left hand.
  • Chopsticks. In Chinese custom, laying chopsticks across the top of your bowl can mean you are ready to leave the table. In Japan, use a chopstick rest, and ask for a fork if you need one.
  • Admiring things. In some Arab homes, a host may offer you an item you praise. Compliment warmly, but don't keep pointing at one thing.
  • Quiet in public. In Japan, people keep voices low on trains and avoid phone calls there.

Gifts and tipping

  • Gifts. A small gift for your host is welcome in many places. What counts as polite, and what to avoid, differs, so ask someone who knows the culture.
  • Tipping in Japan. Tipping is not customary and can confuse staff. A sincere "thank you" is the custom.
  • Tipping in the US. Tips are a big part of pay for servers and other service workers. See the US section below.
  • Before any trip, look up tipping for that country. It ranges from expected to unusual.

How direct people are

In some cultures, people say exactly what they mean. A yes means yes, and prices and deadlines are firm. In others, people rely more on hints, tone and the setting. To avoid an awkward refusal, someone may say "maybe" or go quiet when they mean no.

Neither way is wrong. If you are not sure what someone meant, ask a gentle follow-up question. See how people work for more on reading people.

Everywhere

Some things travel well almost anywhere: a smile, learning to say hello and thank you in the local language, waiting your turn, and following your host's lead. Customs vary a lot within every country, so treat any guide as a starting point. Browse places around the world before a trip.

In the United States

Under federal law, an employer can pay a tipped worker, someone who usually gets more than $30 a month in tips, as little as $2.13 an hour in cash wages, as long as tips bring them up to the federal minimum wage. If they don't, the employer must make up the difference. Many states require more. That is why tipping servers is a strong custom in the US.

In Ohio

In 2026, Ohio's minimum wage is $11.00 an hour, or $5.50 an hour plus tips for tipped workers, according to the Ohio Department of Commerce.
